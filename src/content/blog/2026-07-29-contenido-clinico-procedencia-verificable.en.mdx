# AI Should Not Decide Which Clinical Content Deserves Trust

An exam catalog can contain names, codes, and prices. That does not make it reviewed clinical content.

It also does not prove that the source is still current, that a qualified person evaluated the text, or that the page should appear in search engines.

That boundary looks obvious on a whiteboard. In a system with AI, databases, and automated publishing, it is surprisingly easy to erase.

## The problem starts before the model

When an agent finds a well-structured record, it tends to treat it as available knowledge. But structure is not authority.

A catalog answers operational questions: which exam exists, how it is identified, or how it connects to a quote. Clinical content answers different questions: where the information came from, when it was reviewed, who reviewed it, and how long its source remains current.

Mixing those domains creates a dangerous inference: "if it is in the database, it deserves trust."

AI should not resolve that inference through similarity, statistical confidence, or polished writing. The decision needs explicit and verifiable rules.

## The foundation we validated

On July 26, we implemented and validated a first technical unit that separates those domains.

The main decision was not to add editorial metadata directly to the catalog. Instead, we modeled a separate relationship for the provenance and review of content associated with an exam.

That foundation accounts for data such as:

- the content source and its version;
- the review date;
- the source validity period;
- the reviewer's identity when applicable;
- the state required for deterministic eligibility evaluation.

Validation included an additive migration, schema validation, and four integration tests against a disposable PostgreSQL database. We also verified that historical catalog records were not marked as reviewed by default.

That last point matters: when evidence is missing, the system must preserve an unknown state. It must not invent trust to fill a field.

This is a validated data foundation. It does not mean that a public experience exists, that every catalog record has been reviewed, or that this logic has been deployed.

## The gotcha: a current source is still not enough

A second boundary emerged during design.

Suppose a piece of content has a source, a valid date, and an identified reviewer. It can still be too thin, incomplete, or useless for a publishing decision.

That led to an additional approved policy: insufficient clinical content must fail closed. Correct metadata cannot compensate for weak content.

The conceptual rule is simple:

```text
available catalog data
!= reviewed clinical content
!= current clinical content
!= content eligible for indexing
```

Each transition needs its own evidence. No model should cross those boundaries because the result "looks right."

## What AI should decide

AI can help detect gaps, compare versions, propose classifications, or prepare material for review.

But the authority to turn a record into trusted content should not live inside an opaque inference. It should be represented in the system through states, relationships, and conditions that can be tested.

It is the difference between asking an agent to suggest and allowing it to certify. The first can accelerate work. The second requires authority the model does not have.

## Why this matters beyond Examya

This pattern applies to any product that combines catalogs with sensitive information: medicines, procedures, insurance plans, protocols, or patient education.

The common mistake is to invest first in generation, retrieval, or discovery, then ask which content should have entered the system.

The sequence should be reversed: provenance, review, validity, and sufficiency before distribution.

This is not a clinical, security, compliance, or SEO guarantee. It is a more honest architectural boundary: availability must not be mistaken for trust.

## What comes next

The next step is to connect this foundation to shared eligibility rules and test every case that must remain excluded: expired sources, unverifiable reviewers, incomplete content, or missing review.

Only then does it make sense to discuss public hubs, sitemaps, or discovery experiences. Build the authority of the data first. Decide how to display it second.

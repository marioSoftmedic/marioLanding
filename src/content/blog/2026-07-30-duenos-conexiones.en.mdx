When a platform starts handling more traffic, a familiar answer appears: add replicas or raise the connection limit.

But one earlier question changes the entire calculation: who creates each database client inside every process?

At Examya, we decided to answer that before discussing capacity. Not because we had proved a production incident, but because scaling infrastructure without understanding ownership leaves an important part of the budget unmapped.

## The limit is not the first number

A connection does not appear on its own. A client opens it inside a process running on a replica, and that client shares the budget with other parts of the system.

That is why an isolated “maximum connections” number tells us very little. The calculation must also consider replicas, processes, approved owners, workers, migrations, rolling-deployment overlap, and operating headroom.

Adding replicas without counting owners can multiply potential demand. This is an architectural principle, not a description of an Examya production incident.

## Nine route-local owners

Our Web mapping found nine Prisma clients created locally in routes: five in admin and four in checkout. We also found a separate authentication ownership concern, which we kept outside this work.

The count mattered because it turned a vague suspicion into a concrete inventory. We were no longer debating whether “Prisma uses too many connections.” We were identifying which component owned each client and where that ownership should live.

The finding does not prove exhaustion, higher latency, added cost, or user impact. It proves something narrower: nine route-local owners existed and needed review.

## Consolidating without one large migration

We did not change every route at once. We split the work into bounded local candidates moving toward the canonical `@/lib/prisma` owner.

Six mapped owners were migrated:

- three admin mutation routes;
- two admin read routes;
- one coupon route.

The separation mattered. It let us review smaller contracts instead of combining queries, authorization, formulas, and HTTP responses in a single change whose effects would be harder to attribute.

We are not presenting those migrations as a production improvement. The available evidence does not establish a delivery commit, pull request, deployment, or fewer live connections.

## What the tests support

The candidates had focused evidence: 29/29 in the first admin group, 30/30 cumulatively after adding the read routes, and 5/5 for coupons.

Those tests support behavior preservation within the covered contracts. In particular, the read group preserved the queries, formulas, RBAC rules, and responses included in its scope.

They do not support global correctness, performance, capacity, or production behavior. That distinction matters: a test can show that a refactor preserves a contract without showing that the system now handles more load.

## The gotcha: confusing ownership with capacity

Consolidating clients clarifies the architecture. It does not automatically produce a new connection budget.

To recommend a numeric limit, we still need to measure the real topology: replica and process counts, approved owners, enforced limits, auxiliary jobs competing for connections, and overlap during deployments.

Ownership first. Measurement next. Capacity decisions only after both.

## A diagnostic question for digital health teams

Health platforms often grow while APIs, admin panels, checkout flows, workers, and migrations coexist. Each component may be well tested on its own while still sharing a finite resource.

Before increasing capacity, build a simple inventory:

- process and replica;
- database client owner;
- client lifecycle;
- enforced limit and headroom;
- workers, migrations, and temporary overlap.

Our next step is not to promise a universal number. It is to keep ownership explicit and compare it with measurements from the topology that actually runs.

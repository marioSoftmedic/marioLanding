When a medical center or laboratory decides to "interoperate," the IT team usually assumes the main challenge is learning the HL7 FHIR standard's structure and ensuring their systems send and receive JSONs correctly.

The big surprise hits in production: data crosses the wire without HTTP errors, the payload passes structural validation, but the destination system simply **does not understand what that data means**.

That is the exact border where **syntactic** interoperability dies and the true bottleneck of Chile's Law 21.668 begins: **semantic** interoperability.

### The Container vs. The Meaning

To understand why clinical interoperability projects are delayed for months, we must separate two concepts:

1. **Syntactic Interoperability (The Container):** It's the ability of two systems to exchange data using a common structure. If we use FHIR, we agree that a patient travels in a `Patient` resource and a lab test in an `Observation`. It's like agreeing to talk on the phone and use English grammar.
2. **Semantic Interoperability (The Meaning):** It's the ability to ensure that the shared information is understood in the exact same way by both systems. It's ensuring that if I say "GLIC", you understand that I mean exactly a Blood Glucose test, and nothing else.

### The Clash of Proprietary Dictionaries

Historically, every clinical laboratory and medical center has built its own proprietary dictionary of services.

Laboratory A codes glucose as `GLU-01`. Laboratory B calls it `GLIC_S`. Hospital C uses `BLOOD-GLUCOSE`.

If all three systems expose a FHIR API (syntactic interoperability), the `Observation` resource will travel perfectly structured. But when the doctor's system receives the JSON, their software will read `GLU-01` and won't know how to graph the patient's glycemic history because, in its local database, glucose is named `GLIC`.

The result: the doctor has to read a free-text PDF, breaking the continuity of care and the spirit of Law 21.668.

### The Solution: LOINC and SNOMED CT

Continuity of clinical care cannot be achieved by exchanging dead text. Systems need to map their local catalogs to international standard terminologies.

- **LOINC (Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes):** The global standard for identifying health measurements, observations, and documents. A serum glucose has a unique universal code: `2345-7`.
- **SNOMED CT (Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine - Clinical Terms):** The most comprehensive, multilingual clinical healthcare terminology in the world.

The real integration work isn't building a REST endpoint. The real work, the one that takes weeks of effort, is **semantic mapping**: sitting down with the biochemist and the IT team to standardize the local dictionary (`GLU-01`) to the LOINC standard (`2345-7`).

### Why the Adapter Layer is Crucial

At Fhirex, our experience integrating laboratories taught us that you cannot ask the legacy LIS (Laboratory Information System) vendor to change their entire internal dictionary. You would break their billing and daily operations.

The architectural solution is an intermediate layer (Adapter Layer) that does two things in real-time:
1. **Syntactic Translation:** Transforms the legacy protocol (SOAP, HL7 v2, txt) to FHIR R4.
2. **Semantic Translation:** Maps local LIS codes on the fly to required standards (LOINC/SNOMED CT/ICD-10), ensuring the data leaving for the health network is univocal and machine-processable.

### The Standard is Not Optional

Law 21.668 doesn't aim for clinics to send secure PDFs via email. It aims for a patient's clinical history to be traceable and continuous, regardless of where they are treated. That requires computers to understand the medicine they are transmitting.

If your interoperability project is led solely by engineers worried about the JSON, and doesn't include medical informaticians or biochemists worried about LOINC mapping, your integration is going to hit a semantic wall.
